# Lightweight Self-Supervised Ultrasound Encoder for Differentiating Hepatocellular Carcinoma and Hemangioma on B-Mode Liver Ultrasound

## Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a major global health burden, and early recognition remains clinically important because prognosis worsens once curative treatment is no longer feasible [web:1276]. Ultrasound is widely used as a first-line liver imaging modality because it is inexpensive, accessible, and noninvasive, but grayscale B-mode interpretation is operator dependent and can be difficult when focal liver lesions share overlapping echogenicity and morphology [web:1276][web:1271]. In routine practice, one of the recurring challenges is the distinction between HCC and benign mimickers such as hemangioma on grayscale ultrasound, especially when the lesion is small or atypical [web:1271][web:1276].

Recent deep-learning studies have shown that liver ultrasound images can support automated characterization of focal liver lesions, including benign-versus-malignant discrimination, small-HCC detection, and multiclass hepatic tumor classification [web:1271][web:1028][web:1273]. These studies support the feasibility of ultrasound-based computer-aided diagnosis, but most published pipelines remain predominantly supervised end-task models that optimize classification directly from image-level labels [web:1271][web:1028]. Such a formulation is useful for benchmark performance, yet it does not directly address a central representation-learning question: how to build an ultrasound encoder that preserves diagnostically meaningful patch-level structure and transfers robustly to downstream HCC-focused tasks [web:1274][web:1280].

This limitation is particularly relevant in medical ultrasound. Compared with natural images, B-mode ultrasound contains speckle noise, low contrast, device-dependent appearance variation, and anatomically irrelevant acquisition artifacts, all of which can encourage shortcut learning when supervision is limited [web:1280][web:1276]. A lightweight encoder that can learn stable patch-level representations before downstream supervision may therefore be more clinically useful than a heavy end-to-end classifier trained only for a single terminal label [web:1274][web:1277]. In addition, a compact model is advantageous for ultrasound workflows because it is easier to fine-tune, interpret, and potentially deploy in resource-constrained clinical environments [web:1274][web:1280].

The present study was designed around this representation-first view. Rather than treating HCC-versus-hemangioma classification as the only goal, the study first learns a lightweight self-supervised vision transformer encoder on liver ultrasound and then evaluates whether that encoder provides a favorable starting point for downstream supervised fine-tuning [UNVERIFIED]. The downstream formulation further emphasizes HCC-oriented representation geometry by enabling prototype formation in the latent space, so that HCC similarity can be analyzed at the image or patch level instead of relying solely on a hard binary decision [UNVERIFIED].

Accordingly, the objective of this study is to develop a lightweight self-supervised ultrasound encoder for B-mode liver imaging, to examine its transferability to HCC-versus-hemangioma discrimination, and to establish an HCC prototype-oriented framework that may support clinically interpretable downstream reasoning [UNVERIFIED].

## Relative studies

Deep-learning research on liver ultrasound has expanded in recent years. Prior studies have reported automated characterization of focal liver lesions on abdominal ultrasound, including detection, localization, and lesion classification pipelines using conventional convolutional networks or supervised deep-learning models [web:1276][web:1028]. More recent work has shown that deep learning on grayscale ultrasound can assist in identifying HCC, including small HCC, and can improve classification when auxiliary clinical information such as hepatitis virus status is incorporated [web:1271][web:1273]. These reports indicate that HCC-related classification from ultrasound is feasible, but they still place the main emphasis on end-task prediction accuracy rather than on reusable ultrasound representation learning [web:1271][web:1273].

A parallel line of research has highlighted the promise of self-supervised learning and transformer-based encoders in medical imaging. Self-supervised transformer pretraining has been shown to learn transferable global and local representations in medical images, and vision-transformer literature in ultrasound increasingly supports the value of token-level contextual modeling [web:1274][web:1280]. In pathology and other medical-imaging domains, self-supervised vision transformers have also been associated with interpretable attention patterns and improved downstream transfer, suggesting that transformer encoders can capture meaningful visual concepts beyond simple label fitting [web:1277][web:1274]. However, these studies were not designed specifically for grayscale liver ultrasound discrimination between HCC and hemangioma, nor were they centered on a lightweight encoder that is intentionally constrained for transfer and deployability [web:1274][web:1280].

The present work differs from earlier liver-ultrasound studies in two main ways. First, the primary contribution is not only a downstream classifier but a lightweight stage-1 ultrasound encoder trained to preserve patch-level representation quality for later tasks [UNVERIFIED]. Second, the stage-2 design is motivated by HCC prototype formation: instead of viewing the downstream model solely as a binary decision function, the learned representation is intended to support HCC-centered similarity structure that can later be used for prototype induction, proximity scoring, and patch-level interpretability [UNVERIFIED]. This focus on lightweight self-supervised representation learning plus HCC prototype formation is, to current knowledge, not the central contribution of prior HCC-versus-hemangioma ultrasound studies [web:1271][web:1028][web:1273].

## Methods

### Study design overview

This study used a two-stage learning framework for grayscale B-mode liver ultrasound images [UNVERIFIED]. Stage 1 trained a lightweight self-supervised vision transformer (ViT) encoder to learn transferable patch-level representations from ultrasound without relying solely on terminal classification labels [UNVERIFIED]. Stage 2 evaluated downstream transfer by comparing supervised training from scratch with supervised fine-tuning initialized from the stage-1 encoder, using HCC-versus-hemangioma discrimination as the principal downstream task [UNVERIFIED].

### Dataset and task setting

The study was developed for a liver ultrasound dataset containing B-mode images of hepatocellular carcinoma and hemangioma, a class pair that is clinically relevant because it juxtaposes a common malignant target with a common benign mimic [web:1030]. The emergence of public and structured liver-ultrasound resources such as SMC-LUD supports reproducible development for this task and addresses a longstanding shortage of curated liver-ultrasound datasets [web:1030]. Images were split into training, validation, and test partitions for self-supervised pretraining and downstream supervised evaluation within the project workflow [UNVERIFIED].

### Stage 1: lightweight self-supervised ultrasound encoder

The stage-1 model was a lightweight ViT-based ultrasound encoder designed to retain patch-level information while remaining smaller and easier to fine-tune than larger transformer backbones [UNVERIFIED]. Multi-view self-supervised learning was used so that the encoder could learn view-consistent image representations from global and local crops of the same ultrasound image [UNVERIFIED]. The stage-1 training strategy was built around a DINO-style self-distillation objective, with additional experiments examining masked-image modeling and diversity-promoting components during encoder development [UNVERIFIED]. The practical design goal was to learn encoded patches and image embeddings that remain useful beyond the pretraining task itself [UNVERIFIED].

The lightweight emphasis was intentional. In ultrasound imaging, where datasets are often smaller and domain shift can be substantial across machines and acquisition settings, a compact encoder can reduce optimization burden and make downstream adaptation easier to analyze [web:1280]. A lighter encoder also makes it more feasible to inspect token behavior, attention specialization, and prototype structure during later fine-tuning [UNVERIFIED].

### Stage 2: downstream supervised transfer and HCC-oriented representation shaping

After stage-1 training, the encoder was transferred to stage 2 in a supervised classification framework [UNVERIFIED]. Two downstream regimes were compared: (1) training the same architecture from scratch and (2) fine-tuning from the pretrained stage-1 encoder [UNVERIFIED]. The transferred encoder was fully trainable during stage 2 so that the effect of initialization on downstream optimization could be assessed under a realistic fine-tuning setting [UNVERIFIED].

The downstream model consisted of the encoder, a projection head, and a classification head [UNVERIFIED]. Cross-entropy loss was used for class prediction, and supervised contrastive learning was incorporated to encourage class-aware representation organization in the embedding space [UNVERIFIED]. This combination was chosen to keep stage 2 comparatively simple: the downstream objective was not overloaded with multiple auxiliary tasks, so that any fine-tuning advantage of the stage-1 encoder could be interpreted more directly as a benefit of the learned ultrasound representation [UNVERIFIED].

### HCC prototype perspective

Although the downstream task was implemented as HCC-versus-hemangioma classification, the representation was designed with an HCC prototype perspective in mind [UNVERIFIED]. Prototype-based reasoning has been proposed as a way to relate local image evidence to clinically understandable latent concepts through patch-to-prototype similarity [web:1277]. In the present framework, stage-1 patch representations and stage-2 embedding organization were intended to support later induction of HCC prototypes, allowing the model to express not only binary class membership but also similarity to HCC-centered latent prototypes [UNVERIFIED]. This formulation is clinically relevant because ultrasound diagnosis often involves graded suspicion and differential reasoning rather than a single isolated label [UNVERIFIED].

### Evaluation concept

The main comparison of interest was whether stage-1 pretraining improves downstream fine-tuning relative to training from random initialization under the same stage-2 task definition [UNVERIFIED]. In addition to classification performance, the study design also considered representation-level behavior such as patch-level attention concentration, transfer stability, and the feasibility of later prototype analysis [UNVERIFIED]. Statistical reporting and final performance summaries are described separately in the Results section and are therefore not repeated here [UNVERIFIED].
